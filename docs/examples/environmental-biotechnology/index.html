2. High-Yield Concepts (Must-Memorize Core)

2.1 PGPR — Plant Growth Promoting Rhizobacteria

Definition (verbatim style): PGPR are a heterogeneous group of bacteria found in the rhizosphere, at root surfaces, and in association with roots, which can improve the extent or quality of plant growth directly and/or indirectly.
Direct mechanismsIndirect mechanisms
  1. Producing/altering plant growth regulators: indole-acetic acid (IAA), gibberellic acid, cytokinins, ethylene
  2. Symbiotic N₂ fixation
  3. Solubilization of mineral phosphates and other nutrients
  1. Antagonism against phytopathogens (siderophores, hydrocyanic acid)
  2. Biocontrol via antibiotic production
  3. Triggering induced local or systemic resistance

Genera reported: Pseudomonas, Azospirillum, Azotobacter, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Alcaligenes, Arthrobacter, Burkholderia, Bacillus, Serratia.

2.6 PAHs (Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons)

  • Two or more fused benzene rings, insoluble & stable, carcinogenic.
  • Degradation order (best → worst): 3-ring (89%) > 4-ring (87%) > 5-ring (48%) over 7 weeks.
  • Linear arrangements (anthracene) are more biodegradable than angular (benzo[a]pyrene, pyrene).
  • Degradation requires oxygen insertion into rings — increases solubility & reactivity. PAHs accumulate where O₂ is low.
  • Ligninolytic enzymes attack PAHs because lignin contains similar benzene-ring chemistry.

3. Mechanisms & Reasoning (The "Why")

3.1 Why MSA selects + differentiates S. aureus
⚙ Mechanism: Two layers act simultaneously.
  1. Selection — 7.5% NaCl creates a hyper-osmotic environment. Most bacteria lose water and cannot grow. Staphylococci are halotolerant and survive.
  2. Differentiation — The medium contains mannitol + phenol red indicator. S. aureus ferments mannitol → acid → indicator drops to acidic pH → red turns yellow (a yellow halo around colonies). Coagulase-negative S. epidermidis does not ferment mannitol → colonies stay pink/red on a red background.

Memory hook: "aureus" = Latin aurum = gold = yellow.

3.2 Why MacConkey is selective and differential
⚙ Mechanism:
  • Selection: Crystal violet + bile salts inhibit Gram-positive organisms.
  • Differentiation: Lactose + neutral red indicator. Lactose fermenters (e.g., E. coli) produce acid → bile salts precipitate → neutral red is absorbed → brick-red / pink colonies with a precipitated bile zone. Non-fermenters (Salmonella, Shigella) → colorless / transparent (alkaline reaction).
3.3 Why XLD shows red colonies with black centers
⚙ Three-stage mechanism:
  1. Stage 1 (early): Salmonella ferments xylose → acid → medium turns yellow (just like E. coli, but E. coli further ferments lactose & sucrose, locking the medium yellow → masking).
  2. Stage 2 (xylose exhausted): Salmonella decarboxylates lysine → amines → pH rises again to alkaline → indicator (phenol red) reverts to red.
  3. Stage 3: Salmonella reduces thiosulfate → H₂S, which reacts with ferric ammonium citrate to form a black precipitate (FeS) → black-centered red colonies.
  4. Shigella cannot ferment xylose at all + does NOT make H₂S → plain red colonies.
3.4 Why Baird-Parker shows black colonies + halo for S. aureus
⚙ Mechanism (3 components):
  • Selection: Lithium chloride + 1% potassium tellurite suppress non-staphylococci.
  • Black color: S. aureus reduces tellurite → telluride → grey-to-black colonies.
  • Clear halo: S. aureus produces lecithinase that breaks down lecithin in the egg yolk in the medium → clear "halo" around the black colony.
  • Opaque halo: Coagulase converts fibrinogen (if present) → fibrin → opaque halo (coagulase-positive identification).
3.5 Gram-stain mechanism (and its alternatives)
⚙ Mechanism: Gram⁺ cells have a thick peptidoglycan wall (50–90% of envelope) that traps the crystal violet–iodine complex during decolorization → stay purple. Gram⁻ cells have a thin peptidoglycan layer (~10%) inside an outer membrane that is dissolved by alcohol → CV-I washes out → counterstain with safranin → pink/red.

Steps

  1. Heat-fix smear → apply crystal violet (2 min) — primary stain
  2. Add iodine — mordant; binds CV, traps it inside cell
  3. Rapid decolorization with ethanol/acetone (critical step — over-decolorizing = false Gram-negative)
  4. Counterstain with safranin (2 min)

ONPG nuance: Some "non-lactose fermenters" lack permease but still have β-galactosidase — they show ONPG-positive but ferment lactose slowly = "late lactose fermenter" (e.g., Citrobacter).

3.7 Phosphate solubilization mechanism
⚙ Mechanism: Plants take up P only as orthophosphate (H₂PO₄⁻ at pH < 7; HPO₄²⁻ at pH > 7.2). In soil, P is "locked":
  • In acidic soils: P binds to Fe³⁺ and Al³⁺ surfaces (sorption).
  • In calcareous (alkaline) soils: P precipitates with Ca²⁺ as insoluble calcium phosphates.
Phosphate-Solubilizing Microorganisms (PSMs) — fungi (Aspergillus, Penicillium) and bacteria (Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Rhizobium, Azospirillum) — release organic acids that chelate the cations or lower pH, freeing soluble P.

On agar with insoluble Ca-phosphate, this appears as a clear halo (solubilization zone) around the colony.

3.8 Why API 20E uses 1, 2, 4 (not 1, 2, 3)
⚙ Mechanism (combinatorial logic): 21 tests (20 strip + 1 oxidase) split into 7 triads. In each triad: tube 1 = 1 pt, tube 2 = 2 pts, tube 3 = 4 pts. Sum the positives in each triad → 7-digit profile → look up in API database.

Why 4 and not 3? Because every subset must give a unique sum:

PositivesSum (1,2,3)Sum (1,2,4) ✓

Tube 3 only3 ⚠ same as 1+24

With (1, 2, 4) every combination has a unique sum (0–7). This is the same logic as binary encoding.

Pre-step: Oxidase test must be negative (Enterobacteriaceae are oxidase-negative).

Why add reagents AFTER incubation, not before? Because the reagents could inhibit bacterial growth.

3.9 Why use indicator organisms instead of direct pathogen detection
⚙ Doctor's "pH indicator" analogy: You don't measure every acid in a solution — you use one indicator. Same for water: don't search for every pathogen.

An ideal indicator must be:

  • Present in greater numbers than the pathogen
  • Survive longer (gives time to detect)
  • Non-pathogenic (safe to handle)
  • Easy to test for in routine labs
  • Reflect contamination (present only when water is contaminated)
  • Useful for all water types

4. Practical Protocols Breakdown

4.1 Pour-Plate Procedure (Isolation by Dilution)
  1. Pipette 1 mL or 0.1 mL of a known dilution into the bottom of a sterile Petri dish (before adding media).
  2. Within 20 min, add 12–15 mL of warm (46–48 °C) fluid medium.
  3. Cover the dish.
  4. Swirl gently in a figure-eight pattern (flat on the bench) to disperse the inoculum.
    • Why? Ensures homogeneous distribution; avoids splashing onto the lid.
  5. Allow to cool and solidify.
  6. Invert the plate (medium-side down) and incubate.
    • Why invert? Prevents condensation droplets from pooling on the agar surface — pools could let surface colonies merge or migrate.

Key constraint: Agar must be at 46–48 °C — hot enough to stay liquid, cool enough not to kill the cells.

4.2 Streak-Plate Procedure
  1. Aseptically introduce inoculum to a small area = "the well".
  2. Re-flame loop, cool on remote corner, then streak from the well across one quadrant.
  3. Re-flame after each streak — this dilutes the cell density progressively.
  4. 3–5 streaks per plate; the last quadrant should yield isolated colonies.

Both pour-plate and streak-plate aim to produce single, well-isolated colonies assumed to arise from a single cell — so each colony = one strain.

4.3 Counting rule — the "30–300" Window
  • Lower limit (30): Statistical accuracy minimum.
  • Upper limit (300): Beyond this, accurate counting becomes impossible.
  • Pick the dilution plate that falls in this window for CFU/mL calculation.
4.4 Isolating Pathogenic Staphylococci from Water (full design)
  1. Prepare Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) containing: mannitol (fermentable sugar), phenol red indicator, 7.5% NaCl (selective).
  2. Pipette 1 mL of a known dilution into a sterile Petri dish (before media addition).
  3. Within 20 min, add 12–15 mL warm (46–48 °C) fluid MSA.
  4. Cover & swirl (figure-8) to disperse evenly.
  5. Cool, solidify.
  6. Invert and incubate.
  7. Read: pathogenic S. aureus = small colonies surrounded by yellow zones (mannitol fermentation → acid → phenol red → yellow).
4.5 Isolating Rhizobium from Root Nodules
  1. Locate legume plants in the field; dig a 15-cm radius / 20-cm-deep soil clump around each.
  2. Carefully wash roots under gentle running water — keep nodules on lateral roots intact. Do NOT freeze nodules (ice ruptures bacteroids).
  3. An active N-fixing nodule has a pink, red, or brown interior (leghaemoglobin); ineffective rhizobia → white interiors.
  4. Sever each nodule (~0.5 cm of root either side); use forceps on the appendage, not the nodule itself.
  5. Surface sterilization: 95% ethanol or isopropanol 5–10 s → 2.5–3% sodium hypochlorite 2–4 min → rinse with sterile water.
  6. Crush nodule in a sterile test tube with a sterile glass rod.
  7. Streak one loopful onto YMA + Congo Red; another onto YMA + Bromothymol Blue.
  8. Incubate in the dark (CR can be photoactivated).
  9. Read: White / colorless glistening colonies = Rhizobium; red colonies = contaminants.

YMA composition (per L): K₂HPO₄ 0.5 g; MgSO₄·7H₂O 0.2 g; NaCl 0.1 g; yeast extract 1 g; mannitol 10 g; H₂O up to 1000 mL; pH 6.8–7.0; agar 18 g/L; Congo Red 0.025 g/L. Sterilize 121 °C / 20 min.

🚨 Congo Red Trap: Rhizobium = colorless/white. Red colonies = contaminants. Reverse intuition.
4.6 Phosphate Solubilization Assay
  1. Inoculate isolate centrally onto Pikovskaya / NBRIP medium containing insoluble Ca-phosphate.
  2. Incubate; the colony grows + dissolves nearby insoluble P → clear "solubilization halo".
  3. Measure with a ruler:
    • Diameter of colony (Dcolony)
    • Diameter of solubilization zone (Dzone) — entire halo edge to edge.
  4. Calculate Solubilization Efficiency (formula in §7).
4.7 Bacillus Isolation Protocol
  1. 1 g soil → 9 mL nutrient broth/saline.
  2. Heat 10 min at 80 °C — destroys vegetative bacteria/fungi; spores survive (selects for spore-formers like Bacillus).
  3. Make tenfold serial dilutions (10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁷).
  4. Plate 100 µL aliquots in duplicate on nutrient agar (spread plate).
  5. Incubate 28–30 °C / 24 h.
4.8 Laccase Enzyme Assay (Guaiacol Method)

Reaction mixture:

  • 3.9 mL acetate buffer (10 mmol/L, pH 5.0)
  • 1 mL guaiacol (2 mmol/L)
  • 0.1 mL diluted enzyme solution
  • Incubate 35 °C / 30 min
  • Read absorbance at 470 nm; blank uses buffer instead of guaiacol.

Why guaiacol? Laccase oxidizes it to tetraguaiacol (brown). Color intensity ∝ activity.

(Formula in §7.)

4.9 BOD₅ Procedure
  1. Take two parallel water samples.
  2. Sample A: measure dissolved oxygen immediately (DO₀).
  3. Sample B: incubate in the dark at 20 °C for 5 days; then measure DO₅.
  4. BOD = DO₀ − DO₅ (mg/L).

Why dark? Prevents photosynthesis, which would add O₂ and falsify the result.

4.10 COD Procedure

Reagents: distilled H₂O; 0.250 N K₂Cr₂O₇; conc. H₂SO₄ + Ag₂SO₄; HgSO₄; 0.25 N ferrous ammonium sulfate; ferroin indicator.

  1. 50 mL sample + 1 g HgSO₄ (binds Cl⁻ to prevent interference).
  2. Add 5 mL conc. H₂SO₄; swirl until HgSO₄ dissolves.
  3. In ice bath, slowly add 25 mL of 0.025 N K₂Cr₂O₇ with swirling.
  4. Add 70 mL H₂SO₄–Ag₂SO₄ solution slowly with swirling.
  5. Reflux at 150 °C for 2 hours.
  6. Cool, dilute to ~300 mL with distilled water, cool to room T.
  7. Add 8–10 drops ferroin → titrate excess dichromate with 0.25 N FAS to endpoint (red/brown).
  8. Run a blank in parallel (low-COD water in place of sample).

Mercury is added because chloride is the main interferent (Cl⁻ would consume oxidant via oxidation, falsely raising COD).

6. Visual Specimen Recognition (The Lab Photo Bank)

These are the 19 specimens that appeared in your sample-photos PDF — and per the doctor: "the images used in the exam will be the same ones you saw during lab sessions." Memorize the visual cue → name → mechanism for each.

📷 Phosphate Solubilization Plate (3 photos)

What you see: A Petri dish with a small bacterial colony in the center; around it is a clear / lighter halo (the solubilization zone). A ruler is positioned across the plate.

Identify as: Phosphate Solubilization assay (Pikovskaya / NBRIP-type medium with insoluble Ca-phosphate).

What to do: Measure Dcolony and Dzone with the ruler. Report SE% using the formula.

SE% = [(D_zone − D_colony) / D_colony] × 100

📷 Mannitol Salt Agar (split plate)

What you see: Petri dish split visually — one side YELLOW with growth (Staphylococcus aureus), the other side RED with NO growth (Serratia marcescens).

Identify as: MSA — selective medium.

Reasoning: 7.5% NaCl inhibits non-staph (Serratia); S. aureus ferments mannitol → acid → phenol red turns yellow.

📷 Baird-Parker Agar (Specimen A)

What you see: Two plate views — small black colonies surrounded by clear (lecithinase) halos.

Identify as: Baird-Parker for S. aureus.

Mechanism: Tellurite reduction → black; lecithinase → clear halo; coagulase → opaque inner ring (if present).

📷 Fluorescence-Labeled WGA Method

What you see: Two microscope fields — green-glowing rod cells on the LEFT (Gram-positive); dark-grey, non-fluorescent rods on the RIGHT (Gram-negative).

Identify as: Wheat-germ agglutinin (WGA) fluorescence = alternative to Gram stain.

Mechanism: WGA binds N-acetylglucosamine in the exposed peptidoglycan of Gram-positive cells. In Gram-negative cells, peptidoglycan is hidden under the outer membrane → no labeling.

🚨 Memory aid: Glowing = positive (Gram+). Dark = negative (Gram−).

📷 Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) String Test

What you see: 4-panel image showing a loop being lifted off a slide. Some panels show a thick, viscous string following the loop; others show no string.

Identify as: KOH 3% String Test — Gram alternative.

Read it: Stringy & viscous (within 60 s) = Gram-negative (DNA released by alkali lysis). No string = Gram-positive.

🚨 The classic flip-trap. Positive test (string) ≠ Gram positive! Stringy = Gram-NEGATIVE.

📷 API 20E Strip

What you see: Three strips of microtubes — Positive control (multiple distinct colors), Negative control (mostly uniform/yellow), and Test sample (mixed pattern). A 7-digit code interpretation table is provided.

Identify as: API 20E system — phenotypic identification of Enterobacteriaceae.

To answer:

  1. Compare each tube to the reference chart → mark + or − (and read the oxidase test result for the 21st position).
  2. Group into 7 triads of 3 tubes each.
  3. Within each triad, sum: tube 1 (+) = 1, tube 2 (+) = 2, tube 3 (+) = 4.
  4. Concatenate the 7 group sums → 7-digit profile code.
  5. Look up code in API database for species ID.

Worked example from sheet: Triad results 7-0-3-1-6-4-5 → code 7031645.

⚙ Why 1-2-4? Powers of 2 ensure unique sums for any subset of positives (binary encoding). 1-2-3 would create ambiguity (1+2 = 3 same as tube 3 alone).

📷 XLD Agar

What you see: Red plate covered in many tiny black-centered colonies.

Identify as: XLD agar.

Organism: Salmonella sp.

Mechanism (3-stage): xylose ferment → acid (initial yellow) → lysine decarboxylated to alkaline (back to red) → thiosulfate → H₂S + ferric ammonium citrate → black FeS centers.

Compare: Shigella = plain red, no black; E. coli = yellow to yellow-red.

📷 Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) Agar

What you see: Dark red agar with two streaks. One streak is dark purple/black; the other is green-metallic-sheen.

Identify as: EMB agar.

Read it: Green metallic sheen = E. coli (strong lactose fermenter, very acidic local pH precipitates dye). Purple/blue-black streak = other lactose fermenter (e.g., Enterobacter). Colorless = non-fermenter (Salmonella, Shigella).

📷 Blood Agar

What you see: Reddish blood agar plate with grey/white colonies; around them are clear/colorless zones where the red blood cells have been completely lysed.

Identify as: Blood Agar — differential medium.

Pattern: Clear zone = β-hemolysis (complete lysis) — typical of S. aureus and S. pyogenes.

Other patterns: α = greenish-brown discoloration (partial lysis, e.g. S. pneumoniae); γ = no change (e.g. Neisseria).

📷 ORSAB Selective Supplement

What you see: Plate stained intense BLUE with small blue colonies (matrix is dark blue).

Identify as: ORSAB (Oxacillin Resistance Screening Agar Base).

Organism: MRSA (methicillin-resistant S. aureus).

Mechanism: Oxacillin (2 mg/L) inhibits MSSA. MRSA grows + ferments mannitol → drops pH → aniline blue indicator → intense blue colonies. NaCl (5.5%) + lithium chloride suppress non-staph; polymyxin B suppresses Proteus.

📷 Salmonella-Shigella Agar (SSA)

What you see: Pale/tan plate with abundant colorless colonies that have black centers.

Identify as: SSA.

Organism: Salmonella.

Mechanism: Bile salts + brilliant green + citrate inhibit Gram⁺ & coliforms. Salmonella doesn't ferment lactose (colorless), but reduces thiosulfate → H₂S → black FeS centers via ferric citrate. Shigella = colorless, no black center.

📷 MacConkey Agar (Media for Salmonella)

What you see: Dark red/brown agar with two streaks — both appear red/pink here, indicating lactose-positive growth in this image.

Identify as: MacConkey Agar.

Read: Brick-red/pink colonies = lactose fermenters (E. coli, coliforms). Colorless transparent = non-fermenters (Salmonella, Shigella).

Selective agents: Crystal violet + bile salts (inhibit Gram⁺).

📷 Test-tube Rack — Multiple Tube Fermentation (MTF)

What you see: Rack of cotton-plugged test tubes containing red, yellow, and orange liquids — corresponds to lactose broth + phenol red tubes from MPN setup.

Identify as: MTF / MPN test (presumptive).

Read: Yellow tubes = positive (lactose ferment → acid → red→yellow); also gas in inverted Durham tubes. Red tubes = negative.

Setup: 5-5-5 series (3 sets × 5 tubes inoculated with 10 mL / 1 mL / 0.1 mL of sample). Count yellow tubes per set → look up MPN/100 mL in reference table.

📷 Pour-Plate Series (red plates with colonies)

What you see: Four red Petri dishes with isolated visible colonies dispersed throughout the agar (not just surface) — classic look of a pour-plate dilution series.

Identify as: Pour-plate technique on a colored selective medium (looks like MacConkey or m-Endo from coloration).

Use: Count colonies in the 30–300 range → calculate CFU/mL.

📷 Coliscan Easygel Guide

What you see: Reference plate with numbered colonies: ① dark blue/purple colonies = E. coli; ② pink/red colonies = other coliforms; ③ teal/green colonies = other organisms.

Identify as: Coliscan Easygel — quantitative method that uses two chromogenic substrates simultaneously.

Reasoning: ONPG-type substrate cleaved by β-galactosidase = pink (all coliforms); MUG-type cleaved by β-glucuronidase = blue/purple (E. coli only). When BOTH enzymes act, the colors superimpose → dark blue/purple.

📷 m-Endo Media (membrane filter)

What you see: Pink/red agar with abundant tiny dark/red colonies, some showing a metallic sheen.

Identify as: m-Endo agar — used for membrane-filter method (quantitative coliform detection).

Read: Red colonies with green metallic sheen = total coliforms (lactose fermentation → aldehyde + basic fuchsin/sodium sulfite reaction). Clear/colorless colonies = non-coliforms.

Selective agents: Sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium deoxycholate (inhibit Gram⁺).

📷 Test-Tube Rack — biochemical test series

What you see: Rack of small tubes containing red, pink, orange, and yellow liquids — classic biochemical fermentation tubes.

Identify as: Sugar fermentation / pH-indicator tubes (e.g., phenol-red broth) or part of an MPN run.

Read: Yellow = acid (positive fermentation); red = no fermentation; orange = partial.

7. Calculations Mastery — Every Formula, Solved

🚨 Doctor's Warning: "They might give you a value in milliliters but ask for it in liters, or vice versa. Always write the unit for the final result." Convert before plugging in. Always.

7.1 CFU/mL Formula

CFU/mL = (Number of colonies × Dilution Factor) / Volume plated (mL)

Variable definitions:

  • Number of colonies — counted on the plate; must be in the 30–300 window for accuracy.
  • Dilution Factor (DF) — the reciprocal of the dilution. If the dilution = 10⁻⁵, DF = 10⁵.
  • Volume plated — actual volume pipetted onto the plate (NOT the volume of the broth; usually 0.1 mL or 0.2 mL).

Solved Example 1 — Final-Answers PDF Question 1

For a soil sample, it was initially diluted 1:100. A subsequent 1:10 dilution was prepared. Finally, 0.2 mL of the final dilution was inoculated on a plate; 64 colonies were counted. Calculate CFU/mL.

Step 1 — Total Dilution Factor: DF = 100 × 10 = 1000

Step 2 — Plug in: CFU/mL = (64 × 1000) / 0.2 = 3.2 × 10⁵ CFU/mL

Solved Example 2 — Lab Manual Problem

5 mL of bacterial culture is added to 45 mL of sterile diluent. From this suspension, two serial 1:100 dilutions are made, and 0.1 mL is plated from the last dilution. After incubation, 137 colonies are counted. Calculate CFU/mL.

Step 1 — Initial dilution: final volume / sample volume = 50/5 = 10 ⇒ first DF = 10 (or 1:10).

Step 2 — Two more 1:100 dilutions: 10 × 100 × 100 = 10⁵

Step 3 — Plug in: CFU/mL = (137 × 10⁵) / 0.1 = 1.37 × 10⁸ CFU/mL

🚨 The classic trap: When 5 mL is added to 45 mL of diluent, the total volume is 50 mL (not 45). The dilution = 5/50 = 1:10. Forgetting to add the sample volume gives you 5/45 = 1:9 (wrong) and is the leading "near-miss" multiple-choice option.

Solved Example 3 — Lab Manual Quick Example

The plate of the 10⁶ dilution yielded 130 colonies. Find CFU/mL.

Bacteria/mL = 130 × 10⁶ = 1.3 × 10⁸ CFU/mL (volume plated ≈ 1 mL implied).

7.2 Solubilization Efficiency (SE%) — Phosphate Solubilization

SE% = [(Diameter of solubilization zone − Diameter of colony) / Diameter of colony] × 100

Variable definitions:

  • Dzone — total diameter of the clear halo (edge of clear zone to opposite edge), measured with a ruler.
  • Dcolony — diameter of the colony itself.

Worked Example

A bacterial colony has Dcolony = 0.8 cm and a clear halo with Dzone = 3.2 cm. Calculate SE%.

SE% = [(3.2 − 0.8) / 0.8] × 100 = (2.4 / 0.8) × 100 = 300%

💡 Tip: Always include cm or mm. Whatever unit you use, both diameters must match. Higher SE% = better phosphate solubilizer.

7.3 Laccase Enzyme Activity

U/mL/min = (ΔAbs × 10⁶) / (ε × R × T)

Variable definitions:

  • ΔAbs — Final absorbance − Initial absorbance, at 470 nm.
  • ε — extinction coefficient of laccase product (εmax = 6,740 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹).
  • R — volume of supernatant (enzyme) used, in mL.
  • T — reaction time in minutes.
  • 10⁶ — converts mol → µmol so that activity is in µmol/mL/min = U/mL.

Worked Example

After a 30-min reaction with 0.1 mL enzyme, ΔAbs at 470 nm = 0.404. Calculate laccase activity.

U/mL = (0.404 × 10⁶) / (6,740 × 0.1 × 30)
= 404,000 / 20,220
≈ 20 U/mL

🚨 Units matter! The doctor said: "Always include the unit U/mL or you'll lose points!"

7.4 BOD Calculation

BOD (mg/L) = DO₀ − DO₅
  • DO₀ — dissolved oxygen at day 0 (initial)
  • DO₅ — dissolved oxygen after 5 days at 20 °C, dark

Higher BOD ↔ more pollution (more organic matter for microbes).

Worked Example (instructor's example)

Sample A: BOD = 8 mg/L. Sample B: BOD = 5 mg/L. Which is more polluted?
Sample A — more O₂ was consumed, meaning more organic matter was decomposed.

7.5 COD Calculation

COD (mg/L) = [8000 × N × (A − B)] / S
  • A — mL of FAS (ferrous ammonium sulfate) used to titrate the blank
  • B — mL of FAS used to titrate the sample
  • N — normality of the FAS solution (typically 0.25 N)
  • S — volume of sample (typically 50 mL)
  • 8000 — 8 mg of oxygen per milliequivalent × 1000 mL per L (gives the result in mg/L when S is in mL)

Worked Example

Blank titration: A = 30 mL FAS. Sample titration: B = 20 mL FAS. N = 0.25. Sample volume S = 50 mL. Calculate COD.

COD = [8000 × 0.25 × (30 − 20)] / 50
= [8000 × 0.25 × 10] / 50
= 20,000 / 50
= 400 mg/L Always state the unit!

Trap: writing the formula with 8 instead of 8000 gives 0.4 — off by a factor of 1000. The 1000 converts the sample volume from mL to L.

💡 Doctor's note: COD > BOD almost always, because COD oxidizes everything (organic + inorganic), while BOD only counts what microbes can degrade in 5 days. The ratio BOD/COD tells you how biodegradable the wastewater is.

7.7 Common Calculation Mistakes

  • Forgetting to add sample volume when computing the first dilution (5 mL into 45 mL ⇒ 5/50, not 5/45).
  • Confusing dilution and dilution factor (DF is the reciprocal: 10⁻⁵ ⇒ DF = 10⁵).
  • Wrong volume in denominator — use the volume plated, not the broth volume.
  • Plate outside 30–300 — answer should note this.
  • Missing units — explicit loss of marks.
  • SE%: dividing by Dzone instead of Dcolony. The denominator is the COLONY.

8. Predicted Exam Questions + Model Answers

8.1 Confirmed Question Templates (from sample sheet)

Q1 — Dilution / CFU Problem (template)
Setup: Soil/water diluted in stages → final dilution plated → colonies counted → calculate CFU/mL or CFU/g.
Strategy: (1) Compute total DF by multiplying each step. (2) For first dilution involving "x mL into y mL diluent," remember total = x + y. (3) Plug into CFU = (colonies × DF) / V_plated. (4) Always state units.
Q2 — Define PGPR and mention the mechanisms used by PGPR which directly and indirectly promote plant growth.
Definition: PGPR = Plant growth promoting rhizobacteria — a heterogeneous group found in the rhizosphere, at root surfaces and in association with roots.

Direct growth promotion — PGPR provides plant growth-promoting substances or facilitates uptake of nutrients:
  • (i) Production / change in concentration of plant growth regulators: indole-acetic acid, gibberellic acid, cytokinins, ethylene.
  • (ii) Symbiotic N₂ fixation.
  • (iii) Solubilization of mineral phosphates and other nutrients.
Indirect growth promotion — PGPR lessens or prevents deleterious effects of phytopathogens:
  • (i) Antagonism by production of siderophores, hydrocyanic acid.
  • (ii) Acting as biocontrol agents through antibiotic production, triggering induced local or systemic resistance.
Q3 — Design an experiment to isolate pathogenic staphylococci from a water sample.
  1. Prepare Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) containing mannitol, phenol red indicator (red→yellow at acid pH), and 7.5% NaCl (high salt inhibits non-staph).
  2. Pipette 1 mL of a known dilution into the bottom of a sterile Petri dish (before adding media).
  3. Within 20 min add 12–15 mL warm (46–48 °C) fluid medium.
  4. Cover and swirl gently in a figure-8 to disperse the sample.
  5. Cool, solidify.
  6. Invert the Petri dish (medium-side down) and incubate.
  7. Read after incubation.
Result: Pathogenic S. aureus produces small colonies surrounded by yellow zones.
Reason: S. aureus ferments mannitol → acid → indicator changes red to yellow.
Q4 — Identify the provided specimen (MacConkey Agar) and write short notes.
MacConkey's Agar
  • Selective for: Gram-negative enteric bacteria.
  • Differential: differentiates lactose fermenters from lactose non-fermenters.
  • Inhibitor: Crystal violet + bile salts inhibit Gram-positives.
  • Use: Detection / isolation of dysentery, typhoid, and paratyphoid; differentiating Salmonella typhosa from coliforms; supports Salmonella & Shigella.
  • Brick-red colonies with bile precipitate zone = lactose-fermenting coliforms.
  • Colorless / transparent colonies = non-fermenters (dysentery, typhoid, paratyphoid bacilli).
  • Reason for color: Acid from lactose fermentation acts on bile salts; neutral red is absorbed by the precipitated salts. Transparent ones don't ferment lactose — alkaline reaction.

8.2 Likely Additional Questions (built from the doctor's emphases)

Q5 — Why are E. coli used as the indicator of fecal contamination instead of testing for pathogens directly?
  • Pathogens are usually present in low numbers and are difficult/expensive/unsafe to detect directly.
  • E. coli lives in the intestines of warm-blooded mammals → its presence in water = fecal contamination = high probability of pathogens.
  • Indicator criteria met: present in greater numbers, non-pathogenic, easy to test, reflects environmental change, useful for all water types.
  • Specifically, E. coli uniquely possesses β-glucuronidase, distinguishing it from other coliforms.
Q6 — Identify the medium and specimen: red plate, abundant tiny black-centered colonies on red background. Explain the mechanism.
Medium: XLD agar. Organism: Salmonella.
Mechanism (3-stage): (1) Salmonella ferments xylose initially → yellow. (2) Xylose exhausted → decarboxylates lysine → alkaline reaction → indicator (phenol red) reverts to red. (3) Reduces thiosulfate → H₂S; with ferric ammonium citrate → black FeS precipitate at colony center. Shigella doesn't ferment xylose or produce H₂S — appears as plain red colonies.
Q7 — Define BOD. Describe the procedure and interpret a result.
BOD = amount of oxygen required by microorganisms (typically aerobic) to break down organic materials in a water sample.

Procedure (BOD₅):
  1. Take two parallel samples.
  2. Measure DO of sample 1 immediately (DO₀).
  3. Incubate sample 2 in the dark at 20 °C for 5 days.
  4. Measure DO at day 5 (DO₅).
  5. BOD = DO₀ − DO₅ (mg/L).
Interpretation: BOD = 8 mg/L means more polluted than BOD = 5 mg/L (more organics → more O₂ consumption).
Why dark? Prevents algal photosynthesis from artificially adding O₂.
Q8 — Define COD. Why do we add HgSO₄ and Ag₂SO₄? Give the formula.
COD = amount of oxygen required to chemically oxidize all organic + inorganic matter in water.
HgSO₄ — binds Cl⁻ (the main interferent) to prevent its oxidation falsely raising COD.
Ag₂SO₄ — catalyst that promotes oxidation of straight-chain alcohols and certain organics.
Formula: COD (mg/L) = [8000 × N × (A − B)] / S, where A = blank titration volume, B = sample titration volume, N = FAS normality, S = sample volume.
Q9 — Identify the test (image showing a viscous string from a loop). State what it tells you and the underlying mechanism.
Test: KOH 3% String Test — a Gram-stain alternative.
Result: A loopful of cells from a colony mixed with a drop of 3% KOH on a slide → if the mixture becomes viscous & "strings" within 60 s = positive = Gram-NEGATIVE.
Mechanism: The dilute alkali lyses Gram-negative cells (thin peptidoglycan + lipid outer membrane), releasing chromosomal DNA. The released DNA makes the suspension viscous.
Gram-positive cells resist lysis → no string = negative test.
Q10 — Explain how the API 20E system works and how the 7-digit code is derived.
API 20E is a phenotypic identification kit for Enterobacteriaceae and other non-fastidious Gram-negative rods. The strip has 20 microtubes containing dehydrated substrates. A bacterial suspension rehydrates the substrates → some tubes give color reactions immediately; others need post-incubation reagents and/or mineral oil for anaerobiosis.

Pre-step: oxidase test must be NEGATIVE (Enterobacteriaceae are oxidase-negative).

Scoring (1, 2, 4 logic): The 21 tests (20 + oxidase) are grouped into 7 triads. Inside each triad the tubes are weighted 1, 2, and 4. Sum the positives in each triad → 7 numbers concatenated → 7-digit profile code → look up in API database.

Why 1, 2, 4 (not 1, 2, 3)? To guarantee unique sums for every subset of positive results. With 1+2+3, the sum 3 could mean "tube 3 only" or "tubes 1 + 2" — ambiguous. With 1+2+4 every combination is unique (binary encoding).
Q11 — Explain the Coliscan test. What do blue, pink, and other colors mean?
Principle: Coliscan uses two chromogenic substrates simultaneously, each cleaved by a coliform-specific enzyme: ONPG by β-galactosidase (all coliforms), and a glucuronide substrate by β-glucuronidase (E. coli only).
Color interpretation:
  • Dark blue / purple = E. coli (both enzymes active).
  • Pink / red = other coliforms (β-galactosidase only).
  • Teal / green = other organisms.
Total coliform count = blue/purple + pink/red.
Q12 — A solubilization plate shows Dcolony = 1 cm and Dzone = 4 cm. What is the SE% and what does it mean biologically?
SE% = [(4 − 1) / 1] × 100 = 300%.
Biologically, the bacterium is releasing organic acids that solubilize insoluble Ca-phosphate (or chelate Fe³⁺/Al³⁺ in the medium), freeing soluble orthophosphate. The bigger the halo relative to the colony, the more efficient the strain is at making P bioavailable to plants.
Q13 — Identify the medium (intense blue colonies / blue background). What organism does it select for and how?
Medium: ORSAB. Organism: MRSA.
Oxacillin (2 mg/L) inhibits methicillin-sensitive S. aureus; high salt + lithium chloride suppress non-staph; polymyxin B suppresses Proteus. MRSA grows + ferments mannitol → acid → aniline blue indicator → intense blue colonies.
Q14 — Why does Baird-Parker agar show a clear halo around S. aureus colonies?
Egg yolk in the medium contains lecithin. S. aureus produces lecithinase (a phospholipase) that hydrolyzes lecithin into diglycerides → the precipitated lecithin is dissolved → clear "halo" around the black colony. The black color comes from tellurite reduction. An additional opaque halo indicates coagulase activity (fibrinogen → fibrin).
Q15 — A Gram-stain photo shows pink/red rod-shaped cells. State Gram reaction, shape, and theory of staining.
Gram reaction: Negative (pink/red, counterstained by safranin).
Shape: Bacilli (rods).
Theory: Gram-positive cells have thick peptidoglycan (50–90% of envelope) that traps the crystal violet–iodine complex through alcohol decolorization → stay purple. Gram-negative cells have thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane that is dissolved by alcohol → CV–I washes out → safranin counterstain → pink/red.
Q16 — What is the laccase enzyme assay? Write the formula and one application.
Laccase activity is assayed using guaiacol as substrate. Reaction mix: 3.9 mL acetate buffer (pH 5), 1 mL guaiacol (2 mM), 0.1 mL diluted enzyme, incubated at 35 °C for 30 min, absorbance read at 470 nm. Blank = guaiacol replaced by buffer.
Formula: U/mL/min = (ΔAbs × 10⁶) / (ε × R × T), where ε = 6,740 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹.
Applications: degradation of PAHs, dyes, phenolic compounds in wine, biopulping/bioleaching of paper, biosensors.
Q17 — In an MTF / MPN test, which set of tubes is positive and how do you read it?
Three sets of 5 tubes are inoculated with 10 mL, 1 mL, and 0.1 mL of sample respectively. After 24 h at 35 °C in lactose broth + phenol red:
Positive: tube turns from red (pH 6.8) to yellow (pH 5.2) due to acid from lactose fermentation, AND gas trapped in the inverted Durham tube.
Count yellow/gas-positive tubes per set → look up the combination in an MPN table → result reported as MPN/100 mL. The MPN is a statistical estimate, not an exact count.

9.2 Confusable Concept Pairs

Selective vs Differential
Selective = some can't grow at all (inhibited). Differential = all grow but look different. Most exam media are both.
Total vs Fecal coliforms
Total: 35 °C / environment-wide. Fecal: 44.5 °C / gut origin. E. coli is the gold-standard fecal indicator (β-glucuronidase positive).
Rhizosphere vs Rhizoplane
Rhizosphere = soil volume around the root. Rhizoplane = the root surface itself.
α vs β vs γ hemolysis
α = partial (greenish-brown methemoglobin). β = complete (clear zone). γ = none.
P-A vs MTF vs MF
P-A = qualitative (yes/no). MF = quantitative (count colonies on m-Endo). MTF/MPN = statistical estimate via tube series.
ONPG vs MUG
ONPG → β-galactosidase → yellow → ALL coliforms. MUG → β-glucuronidase → fluorescence → E. coli only.
